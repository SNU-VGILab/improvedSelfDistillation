Abstract

Consistency models have been proposed for fast generative modeling, achieving results competitive with diffusion and flow models. However, these methods exhibit instability and limited reproducibility when training from scratch, motivating subsequent work to explain and stabilize these issues. While these efforts have provided valuable insights, the explanations remain fragmented, and the theoretical relationships remain unclear. In this work, we provide a theoretical examination of consistency models by analyzing them from a flow map-based perspective. This joint analysis clarifies how training and convergence behavior can give rise to degenerate solutions. Building on these insights, we revisit self-distillation as a practical remedy for certain forms of suboptimal convergence. To avoid excessive gradient norms during its optimization, we reformulate its objective empirically, yielding improved Self-Distillation (iSD). We further extend iSD with training-time classifier-free guidance, yielding iSD-T, which improves performance and reproducibility in the studied settings. We demonstrate that iSD extends beyond image generation to diffusion-based policy learning, without reliance on pretrained diffusion models for initialization.

Analysis of Instability

3 Preliminary

Flow Matching. Given a dataset \(X\) with underlying distribution \(p_X\), flow matching models are trained to match the velocity fields of continuous flows, starting from a tractable distribution \(p_Z\). Prior work constructs such flows via an interpolation, \(x_t = \alpha_tx + \sigma_t z\), where \(x\sim p_X\) and \(z\sim p_Z\). With the constructed flow, the flow matching models optimize the squared error between the conditional velocity \(v_t(x_t|x) = \alpha'_tx + \frac{\sigma'_t}{\sigma_t}(x_t - \alpha_tx)\) and a network \(F_\theta(x_t; t)\):

\[\mathcal L_\text{CFM} = \mathbb E_{x,z,t}\left[\|F_\theta(x_t; t) - v_t(x_t|x)\|^2_2\right],\qquad F_\theta(x_t; t)\approx v^*_t(x_t) = \mathbb E_{x|x_t}[v_t(x_t|x)].\]

Flow Map. From flows defined by interpolation, our goal is to draw samples from the target distribution in a few sampling steps. To this end, we adopt a flow map \(f(x_t; t, s)\), which is a mapping between two points \(x_t\) and \(x_s\ (s<t)\) on the same trajectory (Kim et al., 2024; Boffi et al., 2025):

\[f(x_t; t, s) = x_t + \int^s_tv_\tau^*(x_\tau)d\tau = x_s.\]

Training Flow Map. Since the flow map is defined as an integral, direct supervision from scratch is challenging. Recent studies adopt consistency training (Song et al., 2023) to avoid data generation, which can be derived from the Eulerian equation:

\[\partial_t f(x_t; t, s) + v^*_t(x_t)\cdot\nabla_xf(x_t; t, s) = 0.\]

To train a flow map network \(f_\theta(x_t; t, s)\) using this equation, Eulerian distillation (Boffi et al., 2025) formulates the objective as a squared minimization problem:

\[\mathcal L_\mathrm{ED} = \mathbb E_{x,z,t,s}\left[\|\partial_tf_\theta(x_t; t, s) + v^*_t(x_t)\cdot\nabla_xf_\theta(x_t; t, s)\|^2_2\right].\]

For consistency training from scratch, using \(v_t(x_t|x)\) instead of \(v^*_t(x_t)\) is a more common choice:

\[\mathcal L_\mathrm{CT} = \mathbb E\left[\left\|f_\theta(x_t; t, s) - \operatorname{sg}\left[f_\theta(x_t; t, s) - \left[\partial_tf_\theta(x_t; t, s) + {\color{#b4462b}v_t(x_t|x)}\cdot\nabla_xf_\theta(x_t; t, s)\right]\right]\right\|^2_2\right].\]

Since \(\mathcal L_\mathrm{ED}\) involves a Jacobian-vector product (JVP), its optimization requires second-order differentiation, introducing computational overhead. In contrast, consistency distillation \(\mathcal L_\mathrm{CD}\) and consistency training \(\mathcal L_\mathrm{CT}\) use a stop-gradient operation, avoiding this issue. However, they alter gradient dynamics, potentially affecting training stability.

4.1 Flow Map Representation for Analysis

To enable a systematic analysis of training instability in consistency models, we propose a flow map representation for commonly used interpolations, expressed as a one-step Euler solution with a network \(F_\theta\):

\[f_\theta(x_t; t, s) = \nu^{-1}(A_{t,s}'x_t - A_{t,s}F_\theta(x_t; t, s)),\qquad A_{t,s}=\sigma_t\alpha_s - \sigma_s\alpha_t.\]

4.2 Suboptimality and Instability Guidance Velocity

Most consistency training approaches are guided by a conditional velocity. However, this setup can contribute to training instability. To examine its effect, we consider Eulerian distillation with a conditional velocity field. Following Boffi et al. (2025), we refer to this as direct training.

\[\mathcal L_\mathrm{DT} = \mathbb E\left[\|\partial_tf_{\theta}(x_t; t, s) + {\color{#b4462b}v_t(x_t|x)}\cdot\nabla_xf_{\theta}(x_t; t, s)\|^2_2\right].\]

Since the global optimum of Eulerian distillation corresponds to the flow map, we show that direct training does not guarantee convergence to this solution by comparing their stationary conditions.

Proposition 4.2 (Stationary Condition of Direct Training)

A stationary point of direct training satisfies

\[\mathcal S_\mathrm{DT} = \mathcal S_\mathrm{ED} + {\color{#b4462b}C} = 0,\quad {\color{#b4462b}C = \nabla\cdot(\rho_t\Sigma_{\Delta v|x_t}\nabla_x f_\theta)},\]

where \(\mathcal S_\mathrm{ED}\) is the stationary condition of Eulerian distillation, \(\Delta v = v_t(x_t|x) - v^*_t(x_t)\), and \(\Sigma_{\Delta v|x_t}\) is the conditional covariance \(\mathrm{Cov}_{x|x_t}[\Delta v]\). This follows from applying the Euler-Lagrange equation to \(\mathcal L_\mathrm{DT}\). This suggests that training can admit a degenerate flow map (\(\mathcal S_\mathrm{ED} = -C\ne 0\)), which is a suboptimal solution.

Thus, the discrepancy is not only an objective-level mismatch: through the term \(C\), it can actually shift the stationary point and admit degenerate flow maps.

When a stop-gradient operation is applied to \(\mathcal L_\mathrm{DT}\), it coincides with the consistency training objective \(\mathcal L_\mathrm{CT}\). This objective can recover solutions satisfying the Eulerian equation, even when guided by conditional velocity. However, this holds only at the expectation level, and a small batch size can induce a residual gap.

Proposition 4.3 (Expectation-level Realization of Consistency Training)

Let \(L_*f_\theta=\partial_t f_\theta+v_t^*\cdot\nabla_x f_\theta\) denote the Eulerian operator applied to \(f_\theta\). The stationary condition of consistency training is

\[\mathcal S_\mathrm{CT} = L_*f_\theta + {\color{#b4462b}\mathbb E_{x|x_t}[\Delta v\cdot\nabla_x f_\theta]} = 0,\]

when \(\rho_t > 0\). This admits the desired flow map as a fixed-point solution, \(L_*f_\theta = 0\), since the second term vanishes by \(\mathbb E_{x|x_t}[\Delta v] = 0\) in expectation. However, under finite-batch estimation, the empirical residual induced by \(\Delta v\) may remain, which can perturb the fixed-point training dynamics.

Figure 3 shows drift toward a degenerate solution as the batch size decreases, which is consistent with the finite-batch residual in Proposition 4.3.

4.3 Suboptimality and Instability Weight Initializations

We further observe that consistency models with \(s=0\) are sensitive to weight initialization, which can affect reproducibility. Recent approaches rely on initialization with pretrained flow models, where the network is already approximately aligned with target flows. This reduces certain early-stage instability, and is often referred to as a preconditioner (Geng et al., 2025). We find that performance varies with the preconditioner, and reproducing results without known preconditioners can be challenging.

4.4 Suboptimality and Instability Time Condition

Some studies (Geng et al., 2025; Frans et al., 2025) enable training from scratch without a preconditioner. The key difference is that they allow \(s<t\), while others fix \(s=0\). Intuitively, training long-range mappings is more challenging than short-range ones. Under the flow map representation, Eulerian distillation can be written as

\[\mathcal L_\mathrm{ED} = \mathbb E\left[\left\|{\color{#7a838e}A''_{t,s}x_t} + {\color{#0070c0}A'_{t,s}(v^*_t - F_\theta)} - {\color{#b4462b}A_{t,s}\frac{dF_\theta}{dt}}\right\|^2_2\right] = \mathbb E\left[\left\|{\color{#0070c0}v^*_t - F_\theta} - {\color{#b4462b}(t - s)\frac{dF_\theta}{dt}}\right\|^2_2\right].\]
\(\alpha_t = 1-t,\ \sigma_t = t,\ t\in[0,1]\) \(\alpha_t = \cos t,\ \sigma_t = \sin t,\ t\in[0,\pi/2]\)

We observe that \(s\to t\) amplifies the flow matching term \(v^*_t - F_\theta\) since \(A''_{t,t}=A_{t,t}=0\), while \(s\to 0\) amplifies the linearization term involving the JVP \(dF_\theta/dt\). As the JVP is structurally more complex, we hypothesize that it may give rise to more undesired fixed points. This suggests that fixing \(s=0\) makes optimization less stable, while relaxing to \(s<t\) balances the terms and mitigates instability.

Time relaxation reduces loss variance and spikes, which can be interpreted as reduced risk of training divergence, supporting our claim.

Method

5.1 improved Self-Distillation (iSD)

Eulerian Self-Distillation (ESD) jointly trains \(F_\theta(x_t; t, t)\) via flow matching and \(f_\theta(x_t; t, s)\) via Eulerian distillation, guided by its own velocity:

\[\mathcal L_\mathrm{SD} = \mathbb E\left[\|\partial_tf_{\theta}(x_t; t, s) + {\color{#0070c0}F_\theta(x_t; t, t)}\cdot\nabla_xf_\theta(x_t; t, s)\|^2_2\right].\]

Compared to direct training, this utilizes the model-induced marginal velocity \(v^*_t(x_t)\approx F_\theta(x_t; t, t)\) instead of the per-sample conditional velocity \(v_t(x_t|x)\), without requiring teacher networks. This encourages convergence to the marginal flow map and addresses suboptimality in Proposition 4.2.

Reformulation. To enable ESD while incorporating design choices, we propose to reformulate self-distillation \(\mathcal L_\mathrm{SD}\) using the flow map representation. In this case, the guidance velocity \(v_\theta(x_t; t) = F_\theta(x_t; t, t)\) corresponds to the marginal velocity under joint training of flow matching:

\[\mathcal L_\mathrm{SD} = \mathbb E\left[\left\| A''_{t,s}x_t + A'_{t,s}(v_\theta(x_t; t) - F_\theta(x_t; t, s)) - A_{t,s}\frac{dF_\theta(x_t; t, s)}{dt}\right\|^2_2\right].\]

To reduce gradient norms, we consider two stop-gradient placements: only the spatial derivative and the full derivative. Both yield theoretically well-defined objectives admitting the desired fixed points, and we empirically select the latter for lower gradient norm and computational efficiency:

\[\mathcal L_\mathrm{SD\text{-}R} = \mathbb E\left[\|F_\theta(x_t; t, s) - \operatorname{sg}\left[F_\mathrm{tgt}\right]\|^2_2\right],\quad F_\mathrm{tgt} = F_\theta + A''_{t,s}x_t + A'_{t,s}(v_\theta - F_\theta) - A_{t,s}\frac{dF_\theta}{dt}.\]

Consistency training can be viewed as a coupled fixed-point iteration with a parameterized target, whose contraction and global convergence are difficult to establish without additional assumptions. Instead, Appendix B.1 suggests that the objectives are aligned and non-conflicting. We leave contraction and global convergence properties for future work.

5.2 Implementation

Computing JVP. To align with the marginal velocity, \(dF_\theta/dt\) needs to follow \(dF_\theta/dt = \partial_tF_\theta + v_\theta\cdot \nabla_x F_\theta\). Alternatively, we can approximate it using a finite difference. Given a small step \(\epsilon\), we define displaced inputs as \(\hat x_{t\pm\epsilon} = x_t \pm \epsilon\cdot v_\theta\), and approximate \(dF_\theta/dt\approx [F_\theta(\hat x_{t+\epsilon}; t + \epsilon, s) - F_\theta(\hat x_{t-\epsilon}; t - \epsilon, s)] /(2\epsilon)\).

Classifier-free Guidance. Since flow maps parameterize mappings rather than velocities, directly applying CFG does not guarantee mappings along the CFG velocity. We thus separate two extensions of iSD: Post-CFG and Pre-CFG. Post-CFG operates as a classical CFG:

\[\tilde f_\theta(x_t; t, s, c) = \nu^{-1}(A'_{t,s}x_t - A_{t,s} \tilde F_c),\quad \tilde F_c = F_\theta(x_t; t, s, \varnothing) + \omega(F_\theta(x_t; t, s, c) - F_\theta(x_t; t, s, \varnothing)).\]

To ensure that the flow map follows the CFG field, we adopt a training-time CFG formulation (Geng et al., 2025), Pre-CFG. This incorporates CFG directly by replacing flow matching with:

\[\mathcal L_\mathrm{CFM}^\omega = \mathbb E\left[\left\|F_\theta(x_t; t, t, c) - \tilde v_t(x_t|x)\right\|^2_2\right],\qquad \tilde v_t(x_t|x) = F_\varnothing + \omega(v_t(x_t|x) - F_\varnothing),\]

where \(F_\varnothing = \operatorname{sg}[F_\theta(x_t; t, t, \varnothing)]\). We then train the flow map using the following objective:

\[\mathcal L_\mathrm{iSD\text{-}T} = \lambda_1\mathcal L_\mathrm{CFM}^\omega + \lambda_2\mathcal L_\mathrm{SD\text{-}R}.\]

This objective encourages the flow map to align with the ground-truth CFG velocity. We refer to this case as iSD-T (Training-time CFG). We adopt iSD-T for class-conditional settings and vanilla iSD for class-unconditional settings.

  2. repeat
  3. \(z \sim p_Z,\quad x \sim p_X,\quad t, s\gets \tau\)

  11. until Convergence

6.2 Ablation Study

We observe that self-distillation alone underperforms, while incorporating Pre-CFG \(\mathcal L_\text{iSD-T}\) accelerates convergence, improves performance and reproducibility. One possible explanation is that vanilla iSD may trade the finite-batch residual for network approximation error, which can propagate to flow map training. Pre-CFG amplifies the class component and the flow map is trained along this sharpened field, whose task-relevant signal could become more prominent relative to the propagated approximation error.
